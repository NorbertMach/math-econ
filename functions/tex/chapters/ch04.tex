\chapter{Inequalities in Two Variables: Regions in the Plane}

\chref{algebra}{5} solved inequalities in one variable, where the answer is
a set of points on a line --- an interval, or a union of a few. With two variables
the same question has a two-dimensional answer: the solution set of
$2x+3y\le12$ is not a list of numbers but a \emph{region} of the plane, and
it is described by drawing it.

The mechanism is short. The corresponding \emph{equation} $2x+3y=12$ is a
curve, and a curve cuts the plane into pieces. On each piece the expression
$2x+3y-12$ cannot change sign without passing through zero, so its sign is
constant there. One test point per piece therefore settles the whole
region --- the same sign-table logic as in one variable, with curves in
place of points.

Two directions are worth drilling, and examinations favour the second. The
forward direction is: given inequalities, shade the region. The reverse is:
given a shaded region, write down the inequalities that describe it. The
reverse is harder and more useful, because it is what modelling actually
requires --- a feasible set is described in words or in a picture long before
anyone writes the constraints. It is also exactly the skill that the
graphical solution of a linear programme depends on.

\section{Core Rules}

\begin{rulebox}{The solution set is a region}
For an expression $F(x,y)$, the set $\{F(x,y)<0\}$ is bounded by the curve
$\{F(x,y)=0\}$. The recipe:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item \textbf{Draw the boundary} $F(x,y)=0$. It is one of the curves of
        \chref{functions}{2}: a line, circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola.
  \item \textbf{Note which pieces it creates.} A line makes two half-planes;
        a circle makes an inside and an outside; a parabola makes an
        ``above'' and a ``below''.
  \item \textbf{Test one point per piece.} Substituting a single convenient
        point --- the origin whenever it is not on the boundary --- decides
        the sign for that whole piece.
  \item \textbf{Mark the boundary.} Solid if the inequality is
        $\le$ or $\ge$ (the boundary belongs to the set), dashed if it is
        $<$ or $>$ (it does not).
\end{enumerate}
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{The standard regions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item \textbf{Half-plane:} $ax+by\le c$. Draw the line, test the origin.
        With $c>0$ and the origin satisfying it, the region is the side
        containing the origin.
  \item \textbf{Disc and its complement:} $x^{2}+y^{2}\le r^{2}$ is the
        closed disc; $\ge$ gives everything outside. Shifted:
        $(x-x_{0})^{2}+(y-y_{0})^{2}\le r^{2}$.
  \item \textbf{Ellipse interior:} $\dfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}
        +\dfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}\le1$. Shifted:
        $\dfrac{(x-u)^{2}}{a^{2}}+\dfrac{(y-v)^{2}}{b^{2}}\le1$, the
        elliptical disc about $(u,v)$. Given in expanded form, complete the
        square first (\chref{functions}{2}).
  \item \textbf{Above or below a parabola:} $y\ge x^{2}$ is the region
        \emph{inside} the parabola's opening; $y\le x^{2}$ is everything
        outside it. Rewriting an inequality as $y\ge\cdots$ or
        $y\le\cdots$ makes the side unambiguous: ``$y$ at least this'' means
        upwards.
  \item \textbf{Between hyperbola branches:} $xy\ge c$ with $c>0$ is the
        pair of regions beyond the two branches --- and on the positive
        quadrant alone it is the convex ``better set'' of Cobb--Douglas.
  \item \textbf{Shifted hyperbola:} for
        $\dfrac{(x-u)^{2}}{a^{2}}-\dfrac{(y-v)^{2}}{b^{2}}\le1$ the region is
        the connected piece \emph{between} the branches, containing the
        centre $(u,v)$; it is not convex. With $\ge1$ it is the two pieces
        \emph{beyond} the branches: each piece is convex, their union is
        not. The centre is the natural test point, since it never lies on
        the curve.
  \item \textbf{Strip:} $|x|\le a$ is the vertical strip $-a\le x\le a$;
        $|y|\le b$ a horizontal one.
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Systems: intersect}
Several inequalities holding \emph{simultaneously} give the
\textbf{intersection} of their regions --- shade each one and keep only the
overlap. Two consequences:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item An intersection of half-planes is a \textbf{convex polygon}
        (possibly unbounded). This is precisely the feasible region of a
        linear programme, and the reason the optimum of an LP sits at a
        corner.
  \item An intersection of convex regions is always convex, so discs,
        ellipse interiors and half-planes may be combined freely and the
        result stays convex. A \emph{union}, by contrast, usually is not,
        and neither is the outside of a disc.
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Where two conics meet}
Every corner of a region bounded by two curves is a common point of the
two boundary equations. Before sketching, bring each boundary into
standard form: solve a parabola for $y$ (so $x^{2}+4y\le12$ becomes
$y\le3-\tfrac14x^{2}$), divide an ellipse by its right-hand side (so
$4x^{2}+9y^{2}\le36$ becomes $\tfrac{x^{2}}{9}+\tfrac{y^{2}}{4}\le1$). To
find the common points, use the forms in which $x^{2}$ and $y^{2}$ appear
as whole blocks.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item \textbf{Eliminate one square.} If both equations contain $x^{2}$
        only as the block $x^{2}$ (no $x$ term), solve one of them for
        $x^{2}$ and substitute, or subtract a multiple of one equation from
        the other. What is left is an equation in $y$ alone, linear or
        quadratic. (Eliminate $y^{2}$ instead if that is the clean block.)
  \item \textbf{Solve it, then drop the impossible roots.} Substitute each
        root back into the expression for $x^{2}$. A root that makes
        $x^{2}<0$ gives no point at all and is discarded.
  \item \textbf{Keep both signs.} $x^{2}=c>0$ gives \emph{two} points
        $(\pm\sqrt{c},y)$, mirror images in the $y$-axis. $x^{2}=0$ gives a
        single point on the axis, where the curves usually \emph{touch}
        rather than cross.
\end{enumerate}
Two outcomes need a word. If every root is discarded, the curves do not
meet: one lies entirely inside the other (\emph{nested}) or they are
separate, and one test point settles which. If the leftover equation has a
double root, or gives $x^{2}=0$, the curves are \emph{tangent} there.
Example: $x^{2}+y^{2}=13$ and $4x^{2}+9y^{2}=72$. Subtracting four times the
first from the second leaves $5y^{2}=20$, so $y^{2}=4$ and $x^{2}=9$: four
points $(\pm3,\pm2)$. By contrast $x^{2}+y^{2}=16$ and $x^{2}+2y^{2}=8$ give
$y^{2}=-8$: no common point. The ellipse's vertex $(2\sqrt2,0)$ satisfies
$8<16$, so it lies inside the circle, and with it the whole ellipse.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{The reverse direction: from picture to inequalities}
Given a shaded region, work boundary by boundary.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item \textbf{Identify every boundary curve} and write its equation. A
        circle needs its centre and radius; a line two points or an
        intercept and a slope; a parabola its vertex and one further
        point.
  \item \textbf{Fix the direction of each} with a point that is clearly
        inside the shaded region: substitute it and keep the inequality
        sign that comes out true.
  \item \textbf{Join the conditions with ``and''}, one per boundary. A region
        bounded by three curves needs three inequalities.
  \item \textbf{Check a point just outside} each boundary: it must fail
        exactly one condition. If it fails none, a condition is missing.
\end{enumerate}
Read the boundary style too: a dashed boundary means a strict inequality.

Reading curved boundaries off a grid: a parabola with vertex $(h,k)$ is
$y=a(x-h)^{2}+k$, and one more grid point $(x_{1},y_{1})$ on it gives
$a=(y_{1}-k)/(x_{1}-h)^{2}$. A circle with centre $(u,v)$ through a grid
point $(x_{1},y_{1})$ has $r^{2}=(x_{1}-u)^{2}+(y_{1}-v)^{2}$, which need
not be a perfect square. An ellipse needs its centre and the two vertices
that give $a$ and $b$. If the shaded part lies \emph{outside} a curve (below
an upward parabola, outside a circle or ellipse), the inequality simply
points the other way, and a region with such a curved piece on its
boundary is not convex. And one system may
describe \emph{two separate pieces}; to keep only one of them, add a
condition that cuts the other away.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Which of these regions are convex}
One rule settles almost every case. For a function $f$ of one variable:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item $f$ \textbf{convex} $\Longrightarrow$ the region \emph{above} it,
        $\{y\ge f(x)\}$ (its \de{Epigraph}), is a convex set.
  \item $f$ \textbf{concave} $\Longrightarrow$ the region \emph{below} it,
        $\{y\le f(x)\}$ (its \de{Hypograph}), is a convex set.
  \item Both rules work in reverse: the region below is convex
        \emph{exactly} when $f$ is concave, the region above exactly when
        $f$ is convex. A function that is both is a straight line. So the
        region below the graph of a convex function that is not a straight
        line is not convex, and neither is the region above a concave
        function that is not a straight line.
\end{itemize}
The proof is one line: if $y_{i}\ge f(x_{i})$ and $f$ is convex then
$ty_{1}+(1-t)y_{2}\ge tf(x_{1})+(1-t)f(x_{2})\ge f(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2})$.
So $y\ge x^{2}$ is convex and $y\le x^{2}$ is not, while $y\le4-x^{2}$
\emph{is} convex, because $4-x^{2}$ is concave. Add to this that
half-planes, discs and ellipse interiors are convex, that any
\textbf{intersection} of convex sets is convex, and that the
\emph{outside} of a disc and any \emph{union} of separated pieces is not,
and every region in this chapter is decided.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Absolute values in two variables}
Split on the sign, exactly as in one variable, or recognise the standard
shapes:
\[
  |x|+|y|\le c \;\text{(a diamond, or square rotated }45^{\circ}),\qquad
  \max(|x|,|y|)\le c \;\text{(a square)} .
\]
For $|y|\le f(x)$ the region is squeezed between $y=f(x)$ and $y=-f(x)$,
and exists only where $f(x)\ge0$. For $y\ge|x|$ it is the wedge above both
lines $y=x$ and $y=-x$.
\end{rulebox}

\section{Worked Examples}

\begin{worked}{Sketch $S=\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}\le16,\ y\ge0,\ x\le2\}$, then decide whether $\{(x,y):y\ge\sqrt{x}\}$ is convex}
\wstep{Step 1. Take the constraints one at a time, and shade each.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.2ex]
  \item $x^{2}+y^{2}\le16$: the closed disc of radius $4$ about the origin
        --- ``$\le$'' is the inside, because the origin satisfies it
        ($0\le16$).
  \item $y\ge0$: the upper half-plane, boundary included.
  \item $x\le2$: everything to the left of the vertical line $x=2$.
\end{itemize}
\wtrap{Decide inside-versus-outside by testing one point, not by reading
the inequality sign alone. The origin costs nothing and settles the disc
immediately.
For a line not through the origin, any convenient point does.}

\wstep{Step 2. Intersect the three.}
The region is the part of the upper half-disc lying left of $x=2$: a
half-disc with a circular segment removed on the right. Its boundary
consists of three pieces --- the segment of the $x$-axis from $x=-4$ to
$x=2$, the vertical segment $x=2$ from $y=0$ up to $y=\sqrt{16-4}=2\sqrt3$,
and the arc of the circle from $(2,2\sqrt3)$ counter-clockwise round to
$(-4,0)$.

\wstep{Step 3. Find the corner points explicitly.}
Where $x=2$ meets the circle: $4+y^{2}=16$, so $y=2\sqrt3\approx3.46$ (the
negative root is cut off by $y\ge0$). Where $y=0$ meets the circle:
$x=\pm4$, of which $x=4$ is cut off by $x\le2$, leaving $(-4,0)$. And
$(2,0)$ where the two straight boundaries meet. Three corners:
$(-4,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(2,2\sqrt3)$.

\wstep{Step 4. Sketch, and check one interior point.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[geoaxis, width=6.6cm, height=6.6cm,
             xmin=-5.2, xmax=5.2, ymin=-1.2, ymax=5.2,
             xtick={-4,-2,2,4}, ytick={2,3.464},
             yticklabels={$2$,$2\sqrt3$}]
  % the region: under the upper semicircle, above y=0, left of x=2
  \addplot[accent, very thick, domain=-4:2, samples=200,
           fill=regionfill, fill opacity=0.5] {sqrt(16-x^2)} \closedcycle;
  % the discarded part of the disc, for contrast
  \addplot[accent!35, thick, dashed, domain=2:4, samples=80]
           {sqrt(16-x^2)};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (axis cs:-1.2,1.4) {$S$};
  \node[font=\tiny, anchor=south west] at (axis cs:2.1,3.6) {$(2,2\sqrt3)$};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Test $(0,1)$: $0+1\le16$ \checkmark, $1\ge0$ \checkmark, $0\le2$
\checkmark. Inside, as drawn.

\wstep{Step 5. Note that $S$ is convex, and why no picture is needed.}
Each of the three constraint sets is convex (a disc, a half-plane, a
half-plane), and \textbf{an intersection of convex sets is convex}. No
picture is needed for that conclusion, and a picture alone cannot replace
it.

\wstep{Step 6. The second region: $\{y\ge\sqrt{x}\}$. Check the definition.}
The boundary $y=\sqrt{x}$ is a smooth, gently bending curve, and the region
above it looks perfectly well behaved. Test the definition instead. Take the
two boundary points
\[
  P=(0,0),\qquad Q=(4,2),
\]
both in the set, since $0\ge\sqrt0$ and $2\ge\sqrt4$. Their midpoint is
$M=(2,1)$, and
\[
  \sqrt{2}\approx1.414>1 ,
\]
so $M$ violates $y\ge\sqrt{x}$ and lies \emph{outside}. A segment between
two points of the set leaves the set:
\[
  \boxed{\{(x,y):y\ge\sqrt{x}\}\ \text{is not convex.}}
\]

\wstep{Step 7. The principle behind it.}
$\sqrt{x}$ is \textbf{concave}. The set above the graph of a function --- its
epigraph --- is convex exactly when the function is \emph{convex}; the set
below --- the hypograph --- is convex exactly when the function is
\emph{concave}. So $\{y\ge\sqrt{x}\}$ fails while $\{y\le\sqrt{x}\}$
succeeds, and $\{y\ge x^{2}\}$ succeeds while $\{y\le x^{2}\}$ fails.
\wtrap{``Above'' is not a synonym for ``convex''. Match the direction of the
inequality to the curvature of the function: convex function with $\ge$, or
concave function with $\le$. Getting this backwards is a common mistake,
and it recurs in \volref{optimization} where the feasible
set of a constrained problem must be convex for the first-order conditions
to be sufficient.}
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}{Sketch $T=\{(x,y):x^{2}+4y\le12,\ x^{2}+2y^{2}\le18\}$ and find every point where the two boundaries meet}
\wstep{Step 1. Rewrite each boundary in standard form.}
The first inequality contains $y$ only linearly, so solve it for $y$:
\[
  x^{2}+4y\le12\iff y\le3-\tfrac14x^{2}.
\]
Its boundary is the downward parabola with vertex $(0,3)$ and roots
$x=\pm2\sqrt3\approx\pm3.46$. The second is an ellipse; divide by $18$:
\[
  x^{2}+2y^{2}\le18\iff\frac{x^{2}}{18}+\frac{y^{2}}{9}\le1,
\]
centre $(0,0)$, semi-axes $a=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\approx4.24$ and $b=3$.
\wtrap{Dividing by $4$ to isolate $y$ keeps the direction of the inequality
because $4>0$. Had the term been $-4y$, the sign would flip: $x^{2}-4y\le12$
means $y\ge\tfrac14x^{2}-3$, the region \emph{above} an upward parabola.}

\wstep{Step 2. Pick the side of each curve with a test point.}
The origin lies on neither curve: $0\le12$ and $0\le18$ are both true. So
$T$ lies \emph{below} the parabola and \emph{inside} the ellipse.

\wstep{Step 3. Find the common points by eliminating $x^{2}$.}
The parabola gives $x^{2}=12-4y$. Substitute into the ellipse equation:
\[
  (12-4y)+2y^{2}=18\iff 2y^{2}-4y-6=0\iff y^{2}-2y-3=0
  \iff (y-3)(y+1)=0 .
\]
Back into $x^{2}=12-4y$: $y=-1$ gives $x^{2}=16$, so $x=\pm4$, two points
$(\pm4,-1)$. And $y=3$ gives $x^{2}=0$, the single point $(0,3)$: the
vertex of the parabola is also the top vertex of the ellipse, and there the
two curves touch. Check on the ellipse: $16+2=18$ and $0+18=18$.
\checkmark
\wtrap{Keep both signs of $x=\pm\sqrt{16}$; dropping the minus sign loses
half the corners. And always substitute back: had a root given $x^{2}<0$, it
would be no point at all and would have to be discarded.}

\wstep{Step 4. Decide which curve is on top in each $x$-range.}
Compare at $x=2$: the parabola is at $y=2$, the ellipse runs from
$-\sqrt7$ to $\sqrt7\approx2.65$, so the parabola cuts through the ellipse
and $T$ is the vertical segment from $-\sqrt7$ up to $2$. At $x=4.1$,
beyond the meeting points, the parabola is at $3-4.2025\approx-1.20$,
below the ellipse's lowest point there, $-\sqrt{(18-16.81)/2}\approx-0.77$:
nothing is left. So $T$ lives over $-4\le x\le4$ only:
\[
  T=\Bigl\{-4\le x\le4,\ \ -\sqrt{\tfrac{18-x^{2}}{2}}\le y\le
  3-\tfrac14x^{2}\Bigr\} .
\]

\wstep{Step 5. Sketch.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[geoaxis, width=6.6cm, height=6.6cm,
             xmin=-5.2, xmax=5.2, ymin=-3.8, ymax=4.2,
             xtick={-4,-2,2,4}, ytick={-2,2}]
  \addplot[draw=none, name path=par, domain=-4:4, samples=161]{3-x^2/4};
  \addplot[draw=none, name path=low, domain=-4:4, samples=161]{-sqrt(max(0,(18-x^2)/2))};
  \region{par}{low}{}
  \addplot[domain=0:360, samples=181]({4.2426*cos(x)},{3*sin(x)});
  \addplot[domain=-4.8:4.8, samples=161]{3-x^2/4};
  \addplot[only marks, mark=*, mark size=1.3pt] coordinates {(-4,-1)(4,-1)(0,3)};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (axis cs:0,0.3) {$T$};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Corners $(\pm4,-1)$, where the curves cross, and the touching point
$(0,3)$, which belongs to $T$ because both inequalities hold with equality.
Interior check: $(0,-2)$ gives $-8\le12$ and $8\le18$. \checkmark

\wstep{Step 6. Convexity follows directly.}
The ellipse interior is convex, and the region below the graph of the
concave function $3-\tfrac14x^{2}$ is convex; their intersection $T$ is
convex.
\end{worked}

\begin{solutions}
  \item The closed half-plane on or above the line $y=2x-1$. Testing
        $(0,0)$: $0\ge-1$ true, so the origin is in it. Convex.
  \item The closed half-plane on or below $x+y=3$ (through $(3,0)$ and
        $(0,3)$); the origin satisfies it. Convex.
  \item The closed disc of radius $3$ about the origin. Convex.
  \item Everything strictly outside the circle of radius $2$, boundary
        dashed and excluded. \textbf{Not} convex.
  \item The region on or above the parabola $y=x^{2}$ --- inside its
        opening. Convex.
  \item The region on or below the downward parabola $y=4-x^{2}$, which
        includes everything far below it. Since $4-x^{2}$ is
        \emph{concave}, the region below it is \textbf{convex}.
  \item The closed elliptical disc with semi-axes $3$ and $2$. Convex.
  \item The region on or beyond the hyperbola branch $y=1/x$ in the first
        quadrant. Convex (this is the quasiconcavity of $xy$).
  \item The closed vertical strip $-2\le x\le2$. Convex, being the
        intersection of two half-planes.
  \item Requires $x\ge0$ and $-x\le y\le x$: the wedge opening to the right
        between the lines $y=\pm x$. Convex.
  \item The closed disc of radius $3$ centred at $(2,-1)$. Convex.
  \item The region strictly above the hyperbola branch $y=1/x$ for $x>0$,
        boundary dashed. Convex.
  \item The triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(6,0)$, $(0,4)$ --- the
        intersection of three half-planes.
  \item The quadrilateral with vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(2,2)$,
        $(0,2)$: the triangle $x+y\le4$ in the first quadrant, with its top
        cut off by $y\le2$.
  \item The upper half-disc of radius $4$: the semicircular region above
        the $x$-axis.
  \item The same half-disc with everything right of $x=2$ removed --- the
        region shown in figure (a).
  \item The lens between the parabola and the line, from $x=-1$ to $x=2$
        (the boundaries meet where $x^{2}=x+2$, i.e. $x=-1,2$). Convex.
  \item The annulus between the circles of radius $1$ and $3$. Not convex.
  \item The quarter-disc of radius $3$ in the first quadrant. Convex.
  \item The region above the parabola, below $y=4$, right of the $y$-axis:
        the right half of figure (b).
  \item $x^{2}+y^{2}\le16$, \;$y\ge0$, \;$x<2$. The circle and the
        $x$-axis are solid, so those two are non-strict; the vertical
        boundary is dashed, so that one is strict.
  \item $y\ge x^{2}$ and $y\le4$. (Equivalently
        $x^{2}\le y\le 4$, which also forces $-2\le x\le2$ without needing
        a separate condition.)
  \item $\dfrac{x^{2}}{9}+\dfrac{y^{2}}{4}\le1$, \;$x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$: the
        quarter of the elliptical disc in the first quadrant.
  \item $y\ge x^{2}$ and $y\le x+2$.
  \item $1\le x^{2}+y^{2}\le9$, that is $x^{2}+y^{2}\ge1$ \emph{and}
        $x^{2}+y^{2}\le9$.
  \item $2x+3y\le12$, \;$x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$.
  \item $y^{2}\le x$ and $x<4$; equivalently
        $-\sqrt{x}\le y\le\sqrt{x}$ with $x<4$. The parabola opens to the
        right and is solid; the line $x=4$ is dashed.
  \item $|x|+|y|\le2$. Written without absolute values it is the four
        conditions $x+y\le2$, $x-y\le2$, $-x+y\le2$, $-x-y\le2$.
  \item All except (e). Each of (a), (b), (c), (d), (f), (g), (h) is an
        intersection of convex sets: discs and ellipse interiors,
        half-planes, the region above the convex $x^{2}$, the region
        \emph{right} of the parabola $x=y^{2}$ (the same rule with the axes
        exchanged, since $y\mapsto y^{2}$ is convex), and the diamond
        $|x|+|y|\le2$, which is an intersection of four half-planes.
        Only (e), the annulus, fails: it is the disc of radius $3$ with a
        hole, and the segment from $(-2,0)$ to $(2,0)$ runs straight
        through the hole.
  \item A diamond with vertices $(3,0)$, $(0,3)$, $(-3,0)$, $(0,-3)$: the
        square of side $3\sqrt2$ rotated by $45^{\circ}$.
  \item The wedge above both lines $y=x$ and $y=-x$ --- everything at or
        above the V of $y=|x|$, opening upwards with its vertex at the
        origin. Convex.
  \item Squeezed between $y=4-x^{2}$ and $y=-(4-x^{2})$, so it exists only
        where $4-x^{2}\ge0$, i.e. for $-2\le x\le2$. It is the
        lens-shaped region symmetric about both axes.
  \item With absolute values: $\max(|x|,|y|)\le2$, i.e. $|x|\le2$ and
        $|y|\le2$. Without: $-2\le x\le2$ and $-2\le y\le2$, four
        half-planes.
  \item The system describes a \emph{larger} set than intended --- a
        condition is missing. Every boundary of the intended region must
        contribute one inequality; a point outside that satisfies them all
        proves one boundary has been forgotten.
  \item Take two points diametrically opposite each other just outside the
        circle: the segment between them passes through the centre of the
        disc, which is not in the set. So the segment leaves the set and
        convexity fails.
  \item For instance $y\ge x+1$ and $y\le x-1$. Each is a satisfiable
        half-plane, but together they demand $x+1\le y\le x-1$, impossible
        since $x+1>x-1$. The two half-planes are on opposite sides of
        parallel lines.
  \item Let $C_{1},C_{2}$ be convex and take $P,Q\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}$.
        Since $P,Q\in C_{1}$ and $C_{1}$ is convex, the whole segment
        $[P,Q]$ lies in $C_{1}$; the same argument puts it in $C_{2}$.
        Hence $[P,Q]\subseteq C_{1}\cap C_{2}$, which is the definition of
        convexity. (The argument works for any number of sets.)
  \item No. The points $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$ both lie in the union, but the
        midpoint $(0,0)$ lies in neither half-plane. A union of disjoint
        convex sets is essentially never convex.
  \item $x^{2}+y^{2}<4$ \;and\; $y>x$. Both boundaries are dashed: the open
        half-disc lying above the diagonal. (It is convex, being an
        intersection of two convex sets.)
  \item Empty for $c<0$; the single point $(0,0)$ for $c=0$; the closed disc
        of radius $\sqrt{c}$ for $c>0$. The three cases are exactly the
        three things a level \emph{set} of $x^{2}+y^{2}$ can do, and they
        are why a sublevel set can be empty without the inequality being
        malformed.
  \item $3x+4y\le60$, \;$x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$: the triangle with vertices
        $(0,0)$, $(20,0)$ and $(0,15)$.
  \item $3x+4y\le60$, \;$xy\ge12$, \;$x,y>0$: the part of the budget
        triangle lying beyond the indifference curve $y=12/x$. It is the
        intersection of a convex set (the triangle) with another convex set
        (the better set of a quasiconcave utility), hence \textbf{convex}.
  \item With $a$ units of A and $b$ of B: $2a+b\le40$ (labour),
        $a+2b\le30$ (capital), $a\ge0$, $b\ge0$. Four half-planes, so a
        convex polygon.
  \item Vertices: $(0,0)$, $(20,0)$, $(0,15)$, and the intersection of
        $2a+b=40$ with $a+2b=30$. Solving: $b=40-2a$ and
        $a+80-4a=30$, so $a=\tfrac{50}{3}$ and $b=\tfrac{20}{3}$. A linear
        objective has parallel straight level lines; pushing them across a
        convex polygon, the last point of contact is a vertex (or a whole
        edge, if the level lines happen to be parallel to it). Hence
        checking the vertices suffices --- the fundamental theorem of
        linear programming.
  \item The two lines meet where $100-2q=20+q$, i.e. $q=\tfrac{80}{3}$ with
        $p=\tfrac{140}{3}$. The region is the triangle with vertices
        $(0,20)$, $(0,100)$ and
        $\bigl(\tfrac{80}{3},\tfrac{140}{3}\bigr)$, so the largest feasible
        quantity is $q=\tfrac{80}{3}\approx26.7$ --- beyond it no price
        satisfies both buyer and seller, which is exactly the competitive
        equilibrium quantity.
  \item Complete the square:
        $4(x^{2}-4x)+9(y^{2}-2y)\le11$, so
        $4(x-2)^{2}-16+9(y-1)^{2}-9\le11$, i.e.
        $4(x-2)^{2}+9(y-1)^{2}\le36$, and dividing by $36$,
        \[
          \frac{(x-2)^{2}}{9}+\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{4}\le1 .
        \]
        The boundary is the ellipse with centre $(2,1)$, $a=3$, $b=2$,
        reaching from $x=-1$ to $x=5$ and from $y=-1$ to $y=3$. The centre
        satisfies the inequality ($0\le1$), so the region is the
        \textbf{closed elliptical disc}, boundary solid. (The origin is
        inside as well: $0\le11$.) Convex, like every ellipse interior.
  \item Complete the square: $(x^{2}+4x)-4(y^{2}-2y)\ge4$, so
        $(x+2)^{2}-4-4(y-1)^{2}+4\ge4$, i.e.
        $(x+2)^{2}-4(y-1)^{2}\ge4$, and dividing by $4$,
        \[
          \frac{(x+2)^{2}}{4}-(y-1)^{2}\ge1 .
        \]
        The boundary is a hyperbola opening left and right, with centre
        $(-2,1)$, $a=2$, $b=1$, vertices $(-4,1)$ and $(0,1)$, asymptotes
        $y-1=\pm\tfrac12(x+2)$. The centre gives $0\ge1$, false, so the
        region is the pair of pieces \textbf{beyond} the branches: left of
        the left branch and right of the right one, boundary solid. The
        origin gives $0\ge4$, false: it lies between the branches (at
        $y=0$ the right branch passes $x=-2+2\sqrt2\approx0.83$).
        \textbf{Not} convex: the vertices $(-4,1)$ and $(0,1)$ belong to
        the region, but their midpoint, the centre $(-2,1)$, does not.
  \item Complete the square: $9(y^{2}+2y)-4(x^{2}-2x)\le31$, so
        $9(y+1)^{2}-9-4(x-1)^{2}+4\le31$, i.e.
        $9(y+1)^{2}-4(x-1)^{2}\le36$, and dividing by $36$,
        \[
          \frac{(y+1)^{2}}{4}-\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{9}\le1 .
        \]
        The boundary is a hyperbola opening up and down: centre $(1,-1)$,
        $a=3$, $b=2$, vertices $(1,1)$ and $(1,-3)$, asymptotes
        $y+1=\pm\tfrac23(x-1)$. The centre satisfies the inequality, so the
        region lies \textbf{between} the two branches, cut off left and
        right by the strip $-2\le x\le4$. Corners: at $x=-2$ and $x=4$,
        $(x-1)^{2}=9$, so $\tfrac{(y+1)^{2}}{4}=2$ and $y=-1\pm2\sqrt2$.
        The corners are $\bigl(-2,-1\pm2\sqrt2\bigr)$ and
        $\bigl(4,-1\pm2\sqrt2\bigr)$, roughly at heights $1.83$ and
        $-3.83$. \textbf{Not} convex: the two upper corners
        $\bigl(-2,-1+2\sqrt2\bigr)$ and $\bigl(4,-1+2\sqrt2\bigr)$ belong
        to the region, but their midpoint $\bigl(1,-1+2\sqrt2\bigr)$ gives
        $\tfrac{(2\sqrt2)^{2}}{4}-0=2>1$; it lies above the upper vertex
        $(1,1)$, outside. The upper branch is the graph of a strictly
        convex function, and the region below such a graph is not convex.
  \item Complete the square:
        $(x-1)^{2}-1+4\bigl[(y+2)^{2}-4\bigr]\le-1$, so
        $(x-1)^{2}+4(y+2)^{2}\le16$, i.e.
        \[
          \frac{(x-1)^{2}}{16}+\frac{(y+2)^{2}}{4}\le1 ,
        \]
        the closed elliptical disc with centre $(1,-2)$, $a=4$, $b=2$. On
        the line, $y=\tfrac{1-x}{2}$ and so $y+2=\tfrac{5-x}{2}$.
        Substituting:
        $\tfrac{(x-1)^{2}}{16}+\tfrac{(5-x)^{2}}{16}=1$, i.e.
        $(x-1)^{2}+(x-5)^{2}=16$, $2x^{2}-12x+26=16$, $x^{2}-6x+5=0$, so
        $x=1$ or $x=5$. The line meets the ellipse at $(1,0)$ and
        $(5,-2)$, its top and right-hand vertices. The centre gives
        $1+2(-2)=-3\le1$, so the region is the part of the disc on the
        centre's side of the chord: the whole disc minus the cap between
        those two vertices. \textbf{Convex}, as the intersection of an
        ellipse interior and a half-plane.
  \item The ellipse's centre is the midpoint of $(-4,1)$ and $(2,1)$,
        namely $(-1,1)$; then $a=3$ (half the horizontal width) and $b=2$
        (from $(-1,1)$ up to $(-1,3)$). The shaded part is the upper half,
        both boundaries solid:
        \[
          \frac{(x+1)^{2}}{9}+\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{4}\le1,\qquad y\ge1 .
        \]
        Expanded: multiply by $36$, $4(x+1)^{2}+9(y-1)^{2}\le36$, i.e.
        $4x^{2}+9y^{2}+8x-18y-23\le0$, together with $y\ge1$. Check
        inside: $(-1,2)$ gives $\tfrac14\le1$ and $2\ge1$. Check outside:
        $(-1,0)$ fails only $y\ge1$, and $(-1,3.5)$ fails only the ellipse.
  \item The centre is the midpoint of the vertices, $(1,-1)$, and
        $a=2$. The asymptote slope is $\tfrac{b}{a}=2$, so $b=4$, and the
        hyperbola is $\tfrac{(x-1)^{2}}{4}-\tfrac{(y+1)^{2}}{16}=1$. The
        shaded region contains the centre, so it lies between the branches;
        the hyperbola is dashed, so that inequality is strict:
        \[
          \frac{(x-1)^{2}}{4}-\frac{(y+1)^{2}}{16}<1,\qquad -4\le y\le2 .
        \]
        Corners: at $y=2$ and at $y=-4$, $(y+1)^{2}=9$, so
        $\tfrac{(x-1)^{2}}{4}=1+\tfrac{9}{16}=\tfrac{25}{16}$ and
        $x-1=\pm\tfrac52$. The corners are
        $\bigl(-\tfrac32,2\bigr)$, $\bigl(\tfrac72,2\bigr)$,
        $\bigl(-\tfrac32,-4\bigr)$, $\bigl(\tfrac72,-4\bigr)$. Because they
        lie on the dashed curve, the corners themselves are not in the
        region.
  \item The centre is the midpoint $(2,0)$ and $a=2$; the slope gives
        $\tfrac{b}{a}=\tfrac12$, so $b=1$. The hyperbola is
        $\tfrac{(x-2)^{2}}{4}-y^{2}=1$.
        (a) Between the branches lies the centre, which gives $0<1$:
        \[
          \frac{(x-2)^{2}}{4}-y^{2}<1 .
        \]
        (b) On or beyond either branch means
        $\tfrac{(x-2)^{2}}{4}-y^{2}\ge1$, which forces $|x-2|\ge2$, i.e.
        $x\le0$ or $x\ge4$. To keep only the right piece add $x\ge2$:
        \[
          \frac{(x-2)^{2}}{4}-y^{2}\ge1,\qquad x\ge2 .
        \]
        Set (b) is \textbf{convex}: it is $\{x\ge2+2\sqrt{1+y^{2}}\}$, the
        region to the right of a convex function of $y$ (the epigraph rule
        with the axes exchanged). Set (a) is \textbf{not}: $(4.8,1)$ and
        $(4.8,-1)$ give $\tfrac{2.8^{2}}{4}-1=0.96<1$ and belong to it,
        but their midpoint $(4.8,0)$ gives $1.96$, which is not below $1$.
\end{solutions}

\begin{solutions}
  \item The open half-plane strictly below the line $y=\tfrac32x-3$.
        Testing $(0,0)$: $0>6$ is false, so the origin is \emph{not} in the
        region; it lies on the other side.
  \item The intersection of the half-plane on or below $y=5-x$ with the
        half-plane $x\ge1$: an unbounded wedge opening down-right, with
        corner $(1,4)$.
  \item The \emph{open} disc of radius $2$ centred at $(-1,2)$; the
        boundary circle is dashed and excluded.
  \item The closed annulus $1\le x^{2}+y^{2}\le4$ between the two circles.
        Not convex.
  \item The region on or above the parabola $y=x^{2}-4$, whose vertex is
        $(0,-4)$ and which crosses the axis at $x=\pm2$. Convex.
  \item Everything on or below the increasing curve $y=2^{x}$, which
        passes through $(0,1)$ and approaches the $x$-axis to the left. Since
        $2^{x}$ is \emph{convex} and not a straight line, the region below
        it is \textbf{not} convex: $(-3,\tfrac18)$ and $(3,8)$ both satisfy the inequality,
        but their midpoint $(0,4.0625)$ does not, since $2^{0}=1<4.0625$.
  \item The region on or above $y=\ln x$; the logarithm's domain confines
        it to the open right half-plane $x>0$. Since $\ln x$ is
        \emph{concave} and not a straight line, the region above it is
        \textbf{not} convex: the
        boundary points $(0.1,\ln 0.1)$ and $(10,\ln 10)$ have midpoint
        $(5.05,0)$, and $0<\ln 5.05\approx1.62$, so the midpoint is
        outside.
  \item The part of the first quadrant on or below the hyperbola $y=4/x$
        --- the sublevel set $\{xy\le4\}$. \textbf{Not} convex: $(0.5,8)$
        and $(8,0.5)$ both lie on the boundary, but their midpoint
        $(4.25,4.25)$ has $xy\approx18.1>4$. (Equivalently: $4/x$ is convex
        on $x>0$ and not a straight line, so the region below it is not.)
  \item $-2\le x-1\le2$, i.e. the closed vertical strip $-1\le x\le3$.
        Convex.
  \item Everything on or outside the diamond $|x|+|y|=1$. Not convex ---
        the segment from $(1,0)$ to $(-1,0)$ passes through the origin,
        where $|x|+|y|=0<1$.
  \item Strictly between the parabola and the line; they meet where
        $x^{2}=x+6$, i.e. $x=-2$ and $x=3$, so the region spans
        $-2<x<3$. Both boundaries dashed.
  \item $\dfrac{x^{2}}{16}+\dfrac{y^{2}}{4}\le1$ with $y\ge0$: the upper
        half of an elliptical disc with semi-axes $4$ and $2$.
  \item $x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$, \;$3x+4y\le12$ (the line through $(4,0)$ and
        $(0,3)$).
  \item $(x-1)^{2}+(y-1)^{2}\le4$, \;$x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$.
  \item $y\ge x^{2}$ and $y\le 2x+3$; the boundaries meet where
        $x^{2}=2x+3$, i.e. $x=-1$ and $x=3$, so $-1\le x\le3$.
  \item With an absolute value: $|y-1|\le2$. Without: $y\ge-1$ and
        $y\le3$.
  \item $\dfrac{x^{2}}{4}+y^{2}\ge1$ \;and\; $x^{2}+y^{2}\le9$: the disc of
        radius $3$ with the elliptical disc punched out. Not convex.
  \item Yes, bounded: $y$ ranges over $[0,4]$ and $x$ over $[-2,2]$, since
        $y\ge x^{2}$ together with $y\le4$ forces $x^{2}\le4$. The extreme
        points are $(\pm2,4)$ and $(0,0)$.
  \item For example $x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$, \;$x+y\le0$: the first two force
        $x,y\ge0$ and the third then forces $x=y=0$, so the solution set is
        exactly $\{(0,0)\}$.
  \item $x\ge0$, \;$y\ge0$, \;$x+y\ge2$. The boundary consists of the
        ray $\{x=0,\ y\ge2\}$, the segment from $(0,2)$ to $(2,0)$, and the
        ray $\{y=0,\ x\ge2\}$, so there are exactly two corners, $(0,2)$
        and $(2,0)$. The region is unbounded (it contains every large
        point of the quadrant) and convex, being an intersection of three
        half-planes.
  \item $\mathrm{e}^{x}$ is a convex function, and the set lying \emph{on
        or above} the graph of a convex function --- its epigraph --- is a
        convex set: if $y_{1}\ge\mathrm{e}^{x_{1}}$ and
        $y_{2}\ge\mathrm{e}^{x_{2}}$ then for $t\in[0,1]$
        \[
          ty_{1}+(1-t)y_{2}\ \ge\ t\mathrm{e}^{x_{1}}+(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{x_{2}}
          \ \ge\ \mathrm{e}^{tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2}},
        \]
        the last step being exactly convexity of $\mathrm{e}^{x}$.
  \item $\sqrt{x}$ is \emph{concave} and not a straight line, so the
        region \emph{above} it is not convex. Concretely, $(0,0)$ and $(4,2)$ both lie on the
        boundary and so belong to the set, but their midpoint $(2,1)$ does
        not, since $\sqrt2\approx1.414>1$. The set that \emph{is} convex is
        the one below the curve, $\{y\le\sqrt{x},\ x\ge0\}$ --- the
        hypograph of a concave function.
  \item The lines meet where $x+y=10$ and $2x+y=16$, i.e. $x=6$, $y=4$.

        outside the ellipse with $a=3$, $b=\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}\approx2.12$;

        $x$-axis at $2\pm\sqrt5$; and the upper half-plane. The circle's
        centre $(2,0)$ gives $4\ge9$, false, so it is not in the region;
        $(4,0)$ satisfies all three ($16\ge9$, $4\le5$, $0\ge0$).
        Intersections: the circle contains $y$ only as $y^{2}$, so take
        $y^{2}=5-(x-2)^{2}=1+4x-x^{2}$ and substitute:
        $x^{2}+2(1+4x-x^{2})=9$, i.e. $x^{2}-8x+7=0$, $x=1$ or $x=7$. For
        $x=7$: $y^{2}=-20$, discarded. For $x=1$: $y^{2}=4$, and $y\ge0$
        keeps $(1,2)$. The ellipse meets $y=0$ at $(3,0)$, the circle at
        $(2+\sqrt5,0)$. The region is bounded by the ellipse arc from
        $(1,2)$ down to $(3,0)$, the $x$-axis from $(3,0)$ to
        $(2+\sqrt5,0)$, and the circle arc back up to $(1,2)$. Corners
        $(1,2)$, $(3,0)$, $(2+\sqrt5,0)$. Left of $x=1$ the circle runs
        inside the ellipse, so nothing is left there. Bounded, one piece,
        \textbf{not convex}: $(1,2)$ and $(3,0)$ belong, their midpoint
        $(2,1)$ gives $4+2=6<9$. (Second picture below.)
  \item Standard forms: the disc of radius $\sqrt{13}\approx3.61$, and
        $\tfrac{x^{2}}{5}-\tfrac{y^{2}}{5}\ge1$, a hyperbola with vertices
        $(\pm\sqrt5,0)$ and asymptotes $y=\pm x$. The origin gives
        $0\ge5$, false, so the region lies beyond the branches; with
        $x\ge0$, to the right of the right branch. Intersections: add the
        equations, $2x^{2}=18$, $x^{2}=9$, $y^{2}=4$; in the first quadrant
        $(3,2)$. The region is bounded by the $x$-axis from $(\sqrt5,0)$ to
        $(\sqrt{13},0)$, the circle arc up to $(3,2)$, and the hyperbola
        back down to $(\sqrt5,0)$. Corners $(\sqrt5,0)$, $(\sqrt{13},0)$,
        $(3,2)$; $(3,0)$ is inside ($9\le13$, $9\ge5$). Bounded, one
        piece, \textbf{convex}: the region right of the right branch is
        $\{x\ge\sqrt{5+y^{2}}\}$, to the right of a convex function of
        $y$, and it is intersected with a disc and a quadrant.
  \item The circle has centre $(3,3)$ and radius $\sqrt{10}\approx3.16$.
        The centre gives $0\ge10$, false, so the region is on or outside
        the circle; the origin, with $9+9=18\ge10$, belongs. Axis points:
        $y=0$ gives $(x-3)^{2}=1$, $x=2$ or $4$; $x=0$ gives $y=2$ or $4$.
        So the disc covers the segments from $2$ to $4$ on both axes, and
        the arc from $(2,0)$ to $(0,2)$ fences off the corner. The region
        has \textbf{two pieces}: a bounded one with corners $(0,0)$,
        $(2,0)$, $(0,2)$ (two straight edges and an arc), and an unbounded
        one with corners $(4,0)$ and $(0,4)$, bounded by the $x$-axis from
        $4$ on, the $y$-axis from $4$ up, and the far arc between them.
        \textbf{Not convex}: $(0,0)$ and $(6,6)$ belong, their midpoint
        $(3,3)$ is the centre. (Third picture below.)
  \item The boundary $y=(x-1)^{2}$ is the upward parabola with vertex
        $(1,0)$; $(1,2)$ satisfies $2\ge0$, so the region is above it.
        Corners: at $x=0$ the parabola is at $y=1$, at $x=3$ at $y=4$; the
        line $y=5$ meets the parabola only at $x=1\pm\sqrt5\approx-1.24$,
        $3.24$, both outside $[0,3]$, so the whole top edge from $(0,5)$
        to $(3,5)$ is boundary. Corners $(0,1)$, $(0,5)$, $(3,5)$, $(3,4)$,
        with the bottom edge running along the parabola through the
        vertex $(1,0)$. Bounded, one piece, \textbf{convex} (above a convex
        function, inside a box).
  \item Now the region is \emph{below} the same parabola and above the
        $x$-axis, between $x=-1$ and $x=3$; $(0,0.5)$ satisfies
        $0\le0.5\le1$. The parabola touches the $x$-axis at its vertex
        $(1,0)$, so the region pinches there into two parts that share only
        that point: a left part with corners $(-1,0)$, $(-1,4)$, $(1,0)$
        and a right part with corners $(1,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(3,4)$. As a set
        it is still \textbf{one piece}, but the two parts hang together
        only through the single point $(1,0)$. Bounded,
        \textbf{not convex}: $(-1,2)$ and $(3,2)$ belong, their midpoint
        $(1,2)$ gives $2\le0$, false. This is the non-convex side of a
        convex function.
\end{solutions}

\begin{solutions}[resume]
  \item \emph{Parabola:} vertex $(1,-2)$, so $y=a(x-1)^{2}-2$; the point
        $(3,2)$ gives $2=4a-2$, $a=1$: $y=(x-1)^{2}-2=x^{2}-2x-1$.
        \emph{Line:} $y=2$. \emph{Left edge:} the $y$-axis, $x=0$. The
        interior point $(1,0)$ gives $0\ge-2$, $0\le2$, $1\ge0$, so
        \[
          y\ge(x-1)^{2}-2,\qquad y\le2,\qquad x\ge0 .
        \]
        Corners: the parabola meets $y=2$ where $(x-1)^{2}=4$, at $(3,2)$
        (the root $x=-1$ is cut off), and meets $x=0$ at $(0,-1)$; the
        third corner is $(0,2)$. Outside checks: $(1,-2.5)$ fails only the
        parabola, $(1,2.5)$ only $y\le2$, and $(-0.2,1)$ only $x\ge0$
        (the parabola there is at $-0.56\le1$).
  \item \emph{Circle:} centre $(1,1)$ through $(3,2)$, so
        $r^{2}=(3-1)^{2}+(2-1)^{2}=5$ and the boundary is
        $(x-1)^{2}+(y-1)^{2}=5$. The centre is inside, so
        \[
          (x-1)^{2}+(y-1)^{2}\le5,\qquad x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0 ;
        \]
        expanded, $x^{2}+y^{2}-2x-2y-3\le0$. Corners: $y=0$ gives
        $(x-1)^{2}=4$, the corner $(3,0)$; $x=0$ gives $(0,3)$; and
        $(0,0)$. Note that the region bulges past $x=3$ to
        $x=1+\sqrt5\approx3.24$: the rightmost point of the region need
        not be a corner.
        Outside checks: $(2,-0.3)$ fails only $y\ge0$, $(-0.3,2)$ only
        $x\ge0$, $(3,3)$ only the circle ($4+4=8>5$).
  \item \emph{Ellipse:} centre $(0,0)$, vertices $(\pm4,0)$ and $(0,\pm2)$,
        so $\tfrac{x^{2}}{16}+\tfrac{y^{2}}{4}=1$. \emph{Parabola:} vertex
        $(0,-1)$ through $(2,0)$, so $y=ax^{2}-1$ with $0=4a-1$,
        $a=\tfrac14$. \emph{Line:} $x=2$, dashed. The interior point
        $(0,0)$ gives $0\le1$, $0\ge-1$, $0<2$, so
        \[
          \frac{x^{2}}{16}+\frac{y^{2}}{4}\le1,\qquad
          y\ge\tfrac14x^{2}-1,\qquad x<2 ;
        \]
        expanded, $x^{2}+4y^{2}\le16$ and $x^{2}\le4y+4$. Outside checks:
        $(0,2.1)$ fails only the ellipse, $(0,-1.2)$ only the parabola,
        and $(2.2,0.5)$ only $x<2$ (it gives $0.365\le1$ and
        $0.5\ge0.21$).
  \item The two straight boundaries on the axes are $x\ge0$ and $y\ge0$;
        the line through $(5,0)$ and $(0,5)$ is $x+y=5$; the arc is the
        circle of radius $2$ about the origin. The interior point $(2,2)$
        gives $4\le5$ and $8\ge4$: the region is on or \emph{outside} the
        circle.
        \[
          x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0,\qquad x+y\le5,\qquad x^{2}+y^{2}\ge4 .
        \]
        Corners $(2,0)$, $(5,0)$, $(0,5)$, $(0,2)$. Outside checks:
        $(1,1)$ fails only the circle, $(3,3)$ only the line,
        $(-0.2,3)$ only $x\ge0$, $(3,-0.2)$ only $y\ge0$. \textbf{Not
        convex}: $(2,0)$ and $(0,2)$ belong, their midpoint $(1,1)$ does
        not.
  \item \emph{Parabola:} vertex $(1,1)$ through $(3,3)$, so
        $3=a\cdot2^{2}+1$, $a=\tfrac12$:
        $y=\tfrac12(x-1)^{2}+1=\tfrac12x^{2}-x+\tfrac32$. \emph{Lines:}
        $x=-1$, $x=4$ and $y=0$. The interior point $(1,0.5)$ gives
        $0.5\le1$, so the region lies \emph{below} the upward parabola:
        \[
          0\le y\le\tfrac12(x-1)^{2}+1,\qquad -1\le x\le4 .
        \]
        Corners $(-1,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(4,5.5)$, $(-1,3)$. Outside checks:
        $(1,1.2)$ fails only the parabola, $(1,-0.2)$ only $y\ge0$,
        $(-1.2,1)$ only $x\ge-1$, $(4.2,1)$ only $x\le4$. \textbf{Not
        convex}: $(-1,3)$ and $(4,5.5)$ belong, but their midpoint
        $(1.5,4.25)$ lies above the parabola, which is only at $1.125$
        there.
  \item \emph{Hyperbola:} centre $(0,0)$ midway between the vertices, so
        $a=2$; the asymptotes pass through $(2,1)$ and $(4,2)$, slope
        $\tfrac{b}{a}=\tfrac12$, so $b=1$:
        $\tfrac{x^{2}}{4}-y^{2}=1$. The centre is \emph{not} shaded
        ($0\ge1$ is false), so the shading lies beyond the branches.
        \emph{Lines:} $x=\pm4$. Interior points $(\pm3,0)$ give
        $\tfrac94\ge1$:
        \[
          \frac{x^{2}}{4}-y^{2}\ge1,\qquad -4\le x\le4 .
        \]
        Corners: $x=\pm4$ gives $y^{2}=3$, so $(\pm4,\pm\sqrt3)$; plus the
        vertices $(\pm2,0)$ on the curved edges. This single system
        describes \textbf{two} separate pieces, because the hyperbola
        inequality alone already does ($|x|\ge2\sqrt{1+y^{2}}$). Outside
        checks: $(0,0)$ fails only the hyperbola, $(4.2,0)$ only
        $x\le4$. Each piece is convex; their union is not.
  \item \emph{Ellipse:} centre $(0,1)$, the midpoint of $(-3,1)$ and
        $(3,1)$; $a=3$ and $b=2$ (from $(0,1)$ to $(0,3)$), so
        $\tfrac{x^{2}}{9}+\tfrac{(y-1)^{2}}{4}=1$. The interior point
        $(4,1)$ gives $\tfrac{16}{9}\ge1$: the region is outside.
        \emph{Lines:} $y=0$ and $y=2$. These three conditions also hold at
        $(-4,1)$, in the unshaded left piece, so a fourth condition is
        needed to cut it away, for instance $x\ge0$:
        \[
          \frac{x^{2}}{9}+\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{4}\ge1,\qquad 0\le y\le2,
          \qquad x\ge0 .
        \]
        Corners: $y=0$ or $y=2$ gives $\tfrac{x^{2}}{9}=\tfrac34$, so
        $\bigl(\tfrac32\sqrt3,0\bigr)$ and $\bigl(\tfrac32\sqrt3,2\bigr)$,
        $\tfrac32\sqrt3\approx2.60$. The line $x=0$ is not part of the
        drawn boundary; any $x\ge c$ with $-\tfrac32\sqrt3<c\le
        \tfrac32\sqrt3$ would do as well. Outside checks: $(2,1)$ fails
        only the ellipse ($\tfrac49<1$), $(4,2.2)$ only $y\le2$, and
        $(-4,1)$ only $x\ge0$. Unbounded and \textbf{not convex}: the two
        corners belong, their midpoint $\bigl(\tfrac32\sqrt3,1\bigr)$ gives
        $\tfrac34<1$.
\end{solutions}
