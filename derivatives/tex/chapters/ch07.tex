\chapter{Indeterminate Forms and L'H\^opital's Rule}

The limit chapter of the analysis volume resolved $0/0$ by algebra alone:
factor and cancel, or multiply by a conjugate. That was deliberate, since
differentiation was not yet available. It is available now, and it turns the
hardest limits into routine ones.

The tool is \textbf{L'H\^opital's rule}: if a quotient tends to $0/0$ or to
$\infty/\infty$, the limit is unchanged when numerator and denominator are
\emph{each} differentiated. Two words in that sentence account for most errors in
practice. \emph{Each}: the rule is not the quotient rule, and the derivative
of the quotient never appears. And the form must genuinely be
indeterminate: applied to a quotient that is not, the rule quietly returns
the wrong number.

Five further indeterminate forms --- $0\cdot\infty$, $\infty-\infty$,
$0^{0}$, $1^{\infty}$, $\infty^{0}$ --- are not quotients at all, and the
chapter's second skill is converting them into one. The last two rows of
the toolkit are of particular use in economics: the
hierarchy of growth rates that decides which of two economic magnitudes
eventually dominates, and the limit that turns a CES production function
into a Cobb--Douglas one.

\section{Core Rules}

\begin{rulebox}{The seven indeterminate forms}
A limit is \textbf{indeterminate} when the limiting behaviour of the parts
does not determine the answer:
\[
  \frac{0}{0},\qquad \frac{\infty}{\infty},\qquad 0\cdot\infty,\qquad
  \infty-\infty,\qquad 0^{0},\qquad 1^{\infty},\qquad \infty^{0} .
\]
Everything else is determined and must simply be evaluated. In particular
$\dfrac{5}{0^{+}}=+\infty$, $\dfrac{0}{5}=0$, $0^{\infty}=0$ and
$\infty^{\infty}=\infty$ are \emph{not} indeterminate --- and applying
L'H\^opital to them is an error, not a shortcut.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{L'H\^opital's rule}
Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable near $a$ with $g'(x)\neq0$ there. If
\[
  \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \lim_{x\to a}|f(x)|=\lim_{x\to a}|g(x)|=\infty ,
\]
and if $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ exists (finite or
$\pm\infty$), then
\[
  \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} .
\]
The same holds for one-sided limits and for $a=\pm\infty$. Three notes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3ex]
  \item \textbf{Differentiate separately.} $\bigl(f/g\bigr)'$ never
        appears. Writing the quotient rule here is the single most common
        mistake.
  \item \textbf{Check the form first, every time.} After one application
        the new quotient may be determinate --- then just evaluate it. If
        it is still $0/0$, apply the rule again.
  \item \textbf{The converse fails.} If $\lim f'/g'$ does not exist, that
        says nothing about $\lim f/g$; the rule simply does not apply.
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Applying the rule twice --- and where it does not apply}
\[
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2x}-1-2x}{x^{2}}
  \;\overset{0/0}{=}\;\lim_{x\to0}\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{2x}-2}{2x}
  \;\overset{0/0}{=}\;\lim_{x\to0}\frac{4\mathrm{e}^{2x}}{2}=2 .
\]
Two applications, each licensed by re-checking the form. Contrast
\[
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x+1}{x+2}=\frac{1}{2},
\]
which is not indeterminate at all. Differentiating top and bottom here
would give $1/1=1$: the wrong answer, produced by a correct rule applied to
a case it does not cover.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Converting the other five forms}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.4ex]
  \item $\mathbf{0\cdot\infty}$: turn one factor over.
        $f\cdot g=\dfrac{f}{1/g}$ gives $0/0$, or
        $\dfrac{g}{1/f}$ gives $\infty/\infty$; pick whichever
        differentiates more pleasantly. So
        $x\ln x=\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}$, and L'H\^opital gives
        $\dfrac{1/x}{-1/x^{2}}=-x\to0$.
  \item $\mathbf{\infty-\infty}$: put over a common denominator, or
        factor out the dominant term, to produce a quotient.
  \item $\mathbf{0^{0}},\ \mathbf{1^{\infty}},\ \mathbf{\infty^{0}}$:
        \textbf{take logarithms}. Set $y=f(x)^{g(x)}$, so
        $\ln y=g(x)\ln f(x)$, a $0\cdot\infty$ form. Find
        $L=\lim\ln y$ and then the answer is $\mathrm{e}^{L}$ ---
        exponentiating at the end is not optional.
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{The hierarchy of growth rates}
For every $a>0$ and every $b>0$, as $x\to\infty$,
\[
  \ln x \;\ll\; x^{a} \;\ll\; \mathrm{e}^{bx},
\]
where $u\ll v$ means $u/v\to0$. Each comparison is one or more
applications of L'H\^opital:
$\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{a}}\to\dfrac{1/x}{ax^{a-1}}=\dfrac{1}{ax^{a}}\to0$, and
$\dfrac{x^{n}}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}$ needs $n$ applications, each lowering the
power by one, until the numerator is constant. Consequences worth
remembering: no polynomial ever outgrows an exponential, no logarithm ever
catches a positive power, and a factor $\ln x$ never changes which side of
the hierarchy a term sits on.
\end{rulebox}

\begin{rulebox}{Two limits every economist needs}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.4ex]
  \item \textbf{Continuous compounding.} $\left(1+\dfrac{r}{n}\right)^{n}$
        is a $1^{\infty}$ form. Taking logs,
        $n\ln\!\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)
        =\dfrac{\ln(1+r/n)}{1/n}\to r$ by L'H\^opital, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{r}$: the bridge from discrete to continuous interest.
  \item \textbf{CES collapses to Cobb--Douglas.} For
        $Q_{\rho}=\bigl(\alpha K^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho}\bigr)^{1/\rho}$,
        \[
          \ln Q_{\rho}
          =\frac{\ln\bigl(\alpha K^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho}\bigr)}{\rho}
          \;\overset{0/0}{\longrightarrow}\;
          \alpha\ln K+(1-\alpha)\ln L \quad (\rho\to0),
        \]
        so $Q_{\rho}\to K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$. The Cobb--Douglas function
        is the $\rho\to0$ member of the CES family, which is why the two
        appear side by side in every textbook.
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\section{Worked Example}

\begin{worked}{Evaluate $\lim_{x\to0}(\cos x)^{1/x^{2}}$; then show why L'H\^opital fails on $\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x+\sin x}{x}$}
\wstep{Step 1. Name the form before doing anything.}
As $x\to0$: $\cos x\to1$ and $1/x^{2}\to+\infty$, so the expression has the
form $1^{\infty}$. That is \textbf{indeterminate} --- it can be anything.
Compare $1^{n}\to1$, but $(1+\tfrac1n)^{n}\to\mathrm{e}$ and
$(1+\tfrac2n)^{n}\to\mathrm{e}^{2}$: the base approaches $1$ while the
exponent grows without bound, and the outcome depends on the relative rates.
\wtrap{$1^{\infty}$, $0^{0}$ and $\infty^{0}$ are indeterminate;
$0^{\infty}=0$ and $\infty^{\infty}=\infty$ are not. Deciding which case you
are in is the first and most error-prone step, and answering ``$1$'' to
$1^{\infty}$ is a common error.}

\wstep{Step 2. Take logarithms, which converts a power into a product.}
Set $y=(\cos x)^{1/x^{2}}$, so
\[
  \ln y=\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^{2}} .
\]
L'H\^opital applies only to $0/0$ and $\infty/\infty$; the logarithm is what
brings an exponential form into one of them. Here $\ln(\cos 0)=\ln1=0$ and
$x^{2}\to0$: the form is $0/0$. \checkmark

\wstep{Step 3. Differentiate top and bottom --- separately, not as a quotient.}
\[
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^{2}}
  \overset{0/0}{=}
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\dfrac{-\sin x}{\cos x}}{2x}
  =\lim_{x\to0}\frac{-\tan x}{2x} .
\]
\wtrap{L'H\^opital says $\lim\frac{f}{g}=\lim\frac{f'}{g'}$ --- the
derivative of the numerator over the derivative of the denominator. It is
\textbf{not} the quotient rule. Writing
$\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}}$ here is a different (and wrong) calculation, and it
is the most common misapplication of the rule.}

\wstep{Step 4. Re-check the form, then apply the rule again.}
$-\tan0=0$ and $2\cdot0=0$: still $0/0$, so a second application is
licensed.
\[
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{-\tan x}{2x}
  \overset{0/0}{=}
  \lim_{x\to0}\frac{-1/\cos^{2}x}{2}=\frac{-1}{2} .
\]
\wtrap{Verify the indeterminate form \emph{before every single
application}. Applying the rule once more to $\frac{-1/\cos^{2}x}{2}$ --- 
which is $-\tfrac12$, perfectly determinate --- would give
$\frac{-2\sin x/\cos^{3}x}{0}$ and nonsense. Each use needs its own
licence.}

\wstep{Step 5. Undo the logarithm.}
$\ln y\to-\tfrac12$, and the exponential is continuous, so
\[
  y\to\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}\approx0.6065 .
\]
\[
  \boxed{\lim_{x\to0}(\cos x)^{1/x^{2}}=\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}}
\]
\wtrap{The limit of $\ln y$ is not the answer. Exponentiating is a step, not
a formality, and stopping at $-\tfrac12$ is the way this problem is most
often half-solved. Numerical check: at $x=0.1$ the expression is
$0.60649$. \checkmark}

\wstep{Step 6. Now a limit that L'H\^opital's rule cannot resolve.}
\[
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+\sin x}{x} .
\]
The form is $\infty/\infty$, so the rule seems to apply. Differentiating:
\[
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1+\cos x}{1} ,
\]
which \textbf{does not exist} --- $\cos x$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$
for ever.

\wstep{Step 7. Read the rule's fine print, then solve it another way.}
L'H\^opital states: \emph{if} $\lim f'/g'$ exists, \emph{then} it equals
$\lim f/g$. It says nothing when $\lim f'/g'$ fails to exist. So the
computation above establishes nothing at all --- it does not show that the
original limit fails.

Elementary algebra settles it in one line:
\[
  \frac{x+\sin x}{x}=1+\frac{\sin x}{x}
  \xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}1+0=1 ,
\]
since $|\sin x|\le1$ and $x\to\infty$.
\wtrap{A failed L'H\^opital attempt is not evidence. And before reaching for
the rule at all, try dividing through, factoring, or the sandwich rule ---
they are faster and they never have side conditions. L'H\^opital is a
last resort, not a first move.}

\wstep{Step 8. The two limits every economist needs, both of this type.}
\[
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(1+\frac{r}{n}\Bigr)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{r}
  \qquad(\text{continuous compounding}),
\]
\[
  \lim_{\rho\to0}\bigl(\alpha K^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho}\bigr)^{1/\rho}
  =K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}
  \qquad(\text{CES}\to\text{Cobb--Douglas}).
\]
Both are $1^{\infty}$, and both are done exactly as in Steps~2--5: take
logs, apply the rule, exponentiate. The second is the reason Cobb--Douglas
is regarded as a special case of CES rather than a separate assumption.
\end{worked}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solutions}
  \item $0/0$; rule gives $\dfrac{\cos x}{1}\to1$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{1}\to1$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{1/x}{1}\to1$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{\sin x}{2x}$, still $0/0$;
        $\dfrac{\cos x}{2}\to\dfrac12$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{2x}{2x-3}\to\dfrac{4}{1}=4$. (Factoring gives the
        same: $\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x-1)}\to\frac{4}{1}$.)
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{\sec^{2}x-1}{3x^{2}}=\dfrac{\tan^{2}x}{3x^{2}}$,
        and since $\tan x/x\to1$ the limit is $\dfrac13$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to1$.
  \item $0/0$;
        $\dfrac{1/\bigl(2\sqrt{1+x}\bigr)}{1}\to\dfrac12$.
  \item $0/0$; $\dfrac{\cos x}{1}\to\cos\pi=-1$.
  \item $0/0$ three times over:
        $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-1-x}{3x^{2}}\to
        \dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-1}{6x}\to
        \dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{6}=\dfrac16$.
  \item $\infty/\infty$; $\dfrac{1/x}{1}=\dfrac1x\to0$.
  \item $\infty/\infty$; $\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to0$.
  \item Twice: $\dfrac{2x}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to
        \dfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to0$.
  \item $\dfrac{1/x}{1/(2\sqrt{x})}=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}\to0$.
  \item $\dfrac{6x+2}{2x}\to3$; or divide through by $x^{2}$ and read off
        the ratio of leading coefficients.
  \item Rewrite as $\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}$, an $\infty/\infty$ form:
        $\dfrac{1/x}{-1/x^{2}}=-x\to0$.
  \item Ten applications reduce the denominator to the constant $10!$,
        leaving $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{10!}\to\infty$. The exponential
        wins.
  \item $\dfrac{2x/(x^{2}+1)}{1/x}=\dfrac{2x^{2}}{x^{2}+1}\to2$.
  \item $0\cdot\infty$; as $\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}$ it becomes
        $\dfrac{1/x}{-1/x^{2}}=-x\to0$.
  \item Same manoeuvre: $\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{-a}}$ gives
        $\dfrac{1/x}{-ax^{-a-1}}=-\dfrac{x^{a}}{a}\to0$. Any positive
        power beats the logarithm.
  \item Substitute $t=1/x\to0^{+}$:
        $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{t}-1}{t}\to1$.
  \item With $t=1/x$: $\dfrac{\ln(1+3t)}{t}\to
        \dfrac{3/(1+3t)}{1}=3$.
  \item $\infty-\infty$. Multiply by the conjugate:
        $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+x}+x}=
        \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/x}+1}\to\dfrac12$.
  \item Common denominator:
        $\dfrac{\sin x-x}{x\sin x}\to\dfrac{\cos x-1}{\sin x+x\cos x}
        \to\dfrac{-\sin x}{2\cos x-x\sin x}=\dfrac{0}{2}=0$.
  \item $\dfrac{(x-1)-\ln x}{(x-1)\ln x}$, which is $0/0$. One
        application gives $\dfrac{x-1}{x\ln x+x-1}$, still $0/0$; a second
        gives $\dfrac{1}{\ln x+2}\to\dfrac12$.
  \item With $t=1/x\to0^{+}$:
        $\dfrac{\pi/2-\arctan(1/t)}{t}$, and
        $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\arctan(1/t)=-\dfrac{1}{1+t^{2}}$,
        so the quotient is $\dfrac{1/(1+t^{2})}{1}\to1$.
  \item $\ln y=x\ln x\to0$ by problem 19, so $y\to\mathrm{e}^{0}=1$.
  \item $1^{\infty}$: $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1+1/x)}{1/x}$; with $t=1/x$,
        $\dfrac{\ln(1+t)}{t}\to1$, so the limit is $\mathrm{e}$.
  \item The same with $r$ in place of $1$:
        $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1+rt)}{t}\to r$, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{r}$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\to1$, so the limit is $\mathrm{e}$.
        (This is the previous limit read with $x=1/t$.)
  \item $\ln y=x\ln\sin x=\dfrac{\ln\sin x}{1/x}$, an
        $\infty/\infty$ form:
        $\dfrac{\cot x}{-1/x^{2}}=-x^{2}\cot x\to0$ since
        $x\cot x\to1$. Hence the limit is $1$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\to0$, so the limit is $1$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln\cos x}{x^{2}}\to
        \dfrac{-\tan x}{2x}\to-\dfrac12$, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1-1/x)}{1/x}\to-1$, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{-1}$.
  \item At $x=0$ the quotient is $\tfrac12$, a determinate value: neither
        numerator nor denominator tends to $0$ or $\infty$. Differentiating
        both would give $1/1=1$, which is simply wrong. The rule is a
        statement about indeterminate forms only.
  \item The rule gives $\dfrac{1+\cos x}{1}$, which oscillates between $0$
        and $2$ and has no limit --- so the rule is inapplicable
        (its hypothesis that $\lim f'/g'$ exists fails). Correctly:
        $\dfrac{x+\sin x}{x}=1+\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\to1+0=1$.
  \item Applying the rule swaps numerator and denominator and returns to
        the original quotient after two steps --- an endless cycle. Instead
        divide through by $\mathrm{e}^{x}$:
        $\dfrac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2x}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2x}}\to
        \dfrac{1-0}{1+0}=1$.
  \item The student differentiated the \emph{quotient} instead of
        differentiating numerator and denominator separately. L'H\^opital
        requires $\dfrac{f'}{g'}=\dfrac{\cos x}{1}$, giving $1$.
  \item Three applications, ending at
        $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{6}$: the value is $\dfrac16$. (Each step
        must be licensed by re-checking that the form is still $0/0$, which
        it is: $1-1-0=0$ and so on.)
  \item $0/0$, so the rule gives $\dfrac{\cos x}{2x}$. As
        $x\to0^{+}$ this tends to $+\infty$; as $x\to0^{-}$ to
        $-\infty$. The one-sided limits differ, so the two-sided limit does
        \textbf{not} exist.
  \item $\ln\!\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{n}\right]
        =\dfrac{\ln(1+r/n)}{1/n}\to r$ by problem 29, so the expression
        tends to $P\mathrm{e}^{r}$. On the left is interest compounded $n$
        times a year; on the right the same nominal rate compounded
        \emph{continuously}. The gap between them is what the limit
        measures.
  \item Take logs: $\ln Q_{\rho}
        =\dfrac{\ln(\alpha K^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho})}{\rho}$. At
        $\rho=0$ the numerator is $\ln1=0$, so the form is $0/0$.
        Differentiating with respect to $\rho$,
        \[
          \frac{\alpha K^{\rho}\ln K+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho}\ln L}
               {\alpha K^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)L^{\rho}}
          \;\xrightarrow[\rho\to0]{}\;
          \alpha\ln K+(1-\alpha)\ln L ,
        \]
        since $K^{0}=L^{0}=1$. Exponentiating,
        $Q_{\rho}\to K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$.
  \item The exponential dominates: $x^{3}/\mathrm{e}^{0.1x}\to0$ by three
        applications of the rule. The crossing solves
        $x^{3}=\mathrm{e}^{0.1x}$, i.e. $3\ln x=0.1x$; trying values,
        $x=100$ gives $13.8$ against $10$, and $x=120$ gives $14.4$
        against $12$, while $x=200$ gives $15.9$ against $20$. So the
        crossing lies between $x\approx150$ and $x\approx200$ --- late, but
        inevitable.
  \item Since $r>0$, $(1+r)^{-n}\to0$, so the present value tends to
        $\dfrac{1}{r}$. No indeterminate form arises. The limit is the
        \textbf{perpetuity}: a payment of $1$ for ever is worth $1/r$
        today.
  \item $\displaystyle\lim_{q\to0^{+}}\left(q+\frac4q\right)=+\infty$: as
        output shrinks, the fixed cost $4$ is spread over almost nothing,
        so average cost grows without bound. And
        $\dfrac{AC(q)}{q}=1+\dfrac{4}{q^{2}}\to1$, so for large $q$ average
        cost grows essentially like $q$ itself: the fixed cost becomes
        irrelevant and $AC\approx q$.
\end{solutions}

\section{Additional Solutions}

\begin{solutions}
  \item $\dfrac{3\cos3x}{1}\to3$.
  \item $\dfrac{3\cos3x}{5\cos5x}\to\dfrac35$.
  \item $\dfrac{3\mathrm{e}^{3x}}{2\mathrm{e}^{2x}}\to\dfrac32$.
  \item $\dfrac{5x^{4}}{3x^{2}}\to\dfrac53$.
  \item $\dfrac{1/(1+x)}{1}\to1$.
  \item Three times:
        $\dfrac{1-\cos x}{3x^{2}}\to\dfrac{\sin x}{6x}\to
        \dfrac{\cos x}{6}=\dfrac16$.
  \item Four times, or twice with a known limit:
        $\dfrac{-\sin x+x}{4x^{3}}$, and by the previous problem
        $\dfrac{x-\sin x}{x^{3}}\to\dfrac16$, so the limit is
        $\dfrac{1}{4}\cdot\dfrac16=\dfrac{1}{24}$.
  \item $\dfrac{2\ln x\cdot(1/x)}{1}=\dfrac{2\ln x}{x}\to0$ (one more
        application, or the hierarchy).
  \item Three times:
        $\dfrac{3x^{2}}{2\mathrm{e}^{2x}}\to\dfrac{6x}{4\mathrm{e}^{2x}}
        \to\dfrac{6}{8\mathrm{e}^{2x}}\to0$.
  \item $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{x}/(1+\mathrm{e}^{x})}{1}\to1$: for large $x$,
        $\ln(1+\mathrm{e}^{x})\approx x$.
  \item $\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{-1/2}}\to\dfrac{1/x}{-\tfrac12x^{-3/2}}
        =-2\sqrt{x}\to0$.
  \item Not indeterminate as written from the right: $x\to0^{+}$ gives
        $0\cdot\infty$, so rewrite with $t=1/x\to\infty$ as
        $\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{t}\to\infty$. The limit is $+\infty$ ---
        the exponential growth dominates the vanishing factor.
  \item Multiply by the conjugate:
        $\dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+3x}+\sqrt{x^{2}-x}}
        =\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{1+3/x}+\sqrt{1-1/x}}\to\dfrac{4}{2}=2$.
  \item Combine: $\dfrac{\cos x-1}{x^{2}\cos x}$, and since
        $\dfrac{\cos x-1}{x^{2}}\to-\dfrac12$ while $\cos x\to1$, the limit
        is $-\dfrac12$.
  \item $\ln y=-x\ln x\to0$, so the limit is $1$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1+2x)}{x}\to2$, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{2}$.
  \item $\ln y=x\ln\!\dfrac{x+2}{x-1}
        =\dfrac{\ln(1+2/x)-\ln(1-1/x)}{1/x}\to2-(-1)=3$, so the limit is
        $\mathrm{e}^{3}$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln\ln x}{x}\to0$ (the numerator grows slower than
        any power), so the limit is $1$.
  \item $\ln y=\dfrac{\ln x}{\ln x}=1$ for every $x$ in the domain, so the
        limit is $\mathrm{e}$ --- no rule needed, just simplification.
  \item $\mathrm{e}^{x/100}$ eventually wins. The hierarchy
        $x^{a}\ll\mathrm{e}^{bx}$ holds for \emph{every} $a>0$ and
        \emph{every} $b>0$; the constants only postpone the crossing, they
        never prevent it.
  \item Each application lowers the power by one and leaves
        $\mathrm{e}^{x}$ in the denominator:
        $\dfrac{x^{n}}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to\dfrac{nx^{n-1}}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}
        \to\cdots\to\dfrac{n!}{\mathrm{e}^{x}}\to0$. Exactly $n$
        applications.
  \item No: the rule would give $\dfrac{1+\sin x}{1-\sin x}$, which has no
        limit (and whose denominator vanishes repeatedly). Divide by $x$
        instead: $\dfrac{1-\cos x/x}{1+\cos x/x}\to\dfrac{1-0}{1+0}=1$.
  \item Write the ratio as
        $\dfrac{p}{q(p)}\cdot\dfrac{q(p+h)-q(p)}{h}$. The second factor
        tends to $q'(p)=-\varepsilon Ap^{-\varepsilon-1}$, so the limit is
        $\dfrac{p}{Ap^{-\varepsilon}}\cdot
        \bigl(-\varepsilon Ap^{-\varepsilon-1}\bigr)=-\varepsilon$:
        constant, independent of $p$. That is what ``constant elasticity''
        means.
  \item $0/0$ as $r\to0^{+}$; differentiating with respect to $r$ gives
        $\dfrac{Ft\mathrm{e}^{-rt}}{1}\to Ft$. Interpretation: at a
        vanishing interest rate, discounting does nothing, and the total
        interest forgone over the period is just $Ft$ --- the linear,
        ``simple interest'' approximation.
  \item $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}y(t)=\dfrac{K}{1+0}=K$: adoption
        saturates at the ceiling $K$. And
        $\displaystyle\lim_{k\to0^{+}}y(t)=\dfrac{K}{1+1}=\dfrac{K}{2}$ for
        every fixed $t$: with no diffusion speed the path flattens into the
        constant half-way level, never moving away from its starting
        point.
\end{solutions}
